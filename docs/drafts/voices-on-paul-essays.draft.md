## Card: `peter`

**Title (keep):** Peter  
**Summary (keep):** Calls Paul’s letters “scripture,” yet flags them as hard to understand and open to distortion by the unstable.  
**Passages (keep):** 2 Peter 3:15–16

### Expandable essay

**House framing — authorship chip (visible in UI):** House framing: Petrine · Wider–critical: authorship of 2 Peter debated

Prefer lead: *author of 2 Peter (accepted as Peter on this atlas’s house framing)*. Do not ship bare “Peter’s voice” as fact.

The author of 2 Peter (accepted as Peter on this atlas’s house framing) speaks near the end of “our beloved brother Paul” who wrote “according to the wisdom given him,” and of Paul’s letters as among “the other Scriptures” (2 Peter 3:15–16). That is high canonical respect: Paul’s correspondence is already being read with the weight of Scripture.

The same breath adds caution. Some things in those letters are “hard to understand,” which “the ignorant and unstable” twist to their own destruction — “as they do the other Scriptures.” This is affirmation **and** warning together: handle the letters carefully; distortion is a real danger—not an unqualified endorsement of every easy reading of Paul.

**What the text says:** beloved brother; wisdom given; letters counted with the Scriptures; some things hard; unstable people twist them.  
**What this essay does not invent:** a full history of Petrine–Pauline relations beyond this passage, or a claim that every first-century reader found Paul simple.

**Deep-links:** letter compare / 2 Peter if surfaced; timeline late-life context; About & method for how the atlas weighs letters and tradition.

## Card: `james`

**Title (keep):** James and the Jerusalem circle  
**Summary (keep):** Recognition and negotiation — fellowship for the Gentile mission alongside concern for Jewish believers and practice.  
**Passages (keep):** Acts 15; Acts 21:17–26; Galatians 2:9–10

### Expandable essay

James and the Jerusalem circle do not appear in Acts or Galatians as cartoon enemies of Paul. The windows we have show **recognition** of the Gentile mission and **ongoing negotiation** over how Jewish and Gentile believers live together.

**Acts 15 (Jerusalem Council).** Dissension over circumcision brings Paul and Barnabas to Jerusalem. In a public assembly, Peter, James, and the apostles and elders affirm Gentile freedom without requiring circumcision; an apostolic letter goes out to the churches (Acts 15). James’s voice weighs Scripture and pastoral care for scattered believers.

**Acts 21:17–26.** Years later Paul reports to James and the elders in Jerusalem. They rejoice over the Gentile work, then raise concern for Jewish believers zealous for the law. Paul joins purification rites — a negotiated path, not a repudiation of the Gentile gospel.

**Galatians 2:9–10.** Paul recalls that James, Cephas, and John — the “pillars” — gave the right hand of fellowship to him and Barnabas for the Gentile mission, asking that they remember the poor.

**Consensus dating note (align with timeline):** On this atlas’s early-Galatians reading, Galatians 2:1–10 belongs with the Acts 11 famine-relief / private pillars meeting (`famine-relief-jerusalem`), **distinct** from the later public Acts 15 council (`jerusalem-council`). Wider Protestant chronologies that date Galatians later often align Galatians 2 with Acts 15. Do not flatten the two into one slide labeled as undisputed fact.

**Frame:** Fellowship for the nations alongside care for Jewish practice — complementary windows, not a single “they all agreed” cartoon.

**Deep-links:** `famine-relief-jerusalem`, `jerusalem-council`, `jerusalem-arrest`; city `jerusalem`; letter Galatians; themes jew-gentile unity if wired.

## Card: `luke`

**Title (keep):** Luke / Acts  
**Summary (keep):** Sympathetic companion narrative — not hagiography; conflict, arrest, and defense scenes stay in view.  
**Passages (keep):** Acts 9; Acts 15; Acts 28

### Expandable essay

Luke’s Acts is this atlas’s **primary travel-and-conflict spine**. It is a sympathetic companion narrative — traditionally linked to the “we” passages — but it is not a stained-glass saint’s life. Conversion, council, riot, arrest, shipwreck, and defense speeches stay on the page.

**Acts 9** opens the hinge: Saul the persecutor confronted by the risen Jesus, baptized, and proclaiming in Damascus.  
**Acts 15** shows Paul inside the Jerusalem negotiation over Gentile inclusion.  
**Acts 28** ends with Paul in Rome under guard, proclaiming “with all boldness and without hindrance” — an open ending, not a martyrdom chapter.

Read Luke beside Paul’s letters as a **second voice**: narrative sequence and public scenes here; personal argument and pastoral charge in the letters. Where chronology is reconstructed (years, journey labels), keep Consensus / Wider labels the timeline already uses. Do not treat Acts’ silence (e.g. Spain, Nero’s sword) as if Luke narrated them.

**Deep-links:** timeline events across the Acts arc; cities along the journeys; After Acts essay for the open ending at Rome; About & method for Acts-as-spine.

## Card: `jude`

**Title (keep):** Jude  
**Note (keep):** Debated link to Pauline tradition  
**Summary (keep):** Shared early-church warning language; any direct “about Paul” link stays labeled as debated, not settled.  
**Passages (keep):** Jude 17–18

### Expandable essay

Jude urges readers to remember “the predictions of the apostles of our Lord Jesus Christ,” who said that in the last time there would be scoffers following ungodly passions (Jude 17–18). The warning vocabulary overlaps language also found in 2 Peter 3:3 — shared early-church concern about scoffers and distortion.

**Confidence label: debated.** Include Jude for completeness among “other voices,” but do **not** present a tight “Jude on Paul” biography. Any direct link from Jude to Pauline tradition or to 2 Peter’s Paul paragraph stays marked **debated**, not settled. This card is about shared warning language in the early church, not a clear second witness to Paul’s character.

**What the text says:** remember the apostles’ predictions; scoffers in the last time.  
**What reconstruction must not claim here:** that Jude is writing a commentary on Paul, or that the Pauline connection is undisputed.

**Deep-links:** optional cross-ref to Peter card (2 Peter 3) with the debate label intact; About & method.

## Card: `opponents`

**Title (keep):** Opponents  
**Summary (keep):** Rivals cast Paul as crafty, bold on paper, weak in person — while some preach Christ from envy; Paul answers without erasing the conflict.  
**Passages (keep):** 2 Corinthians 10–13; Galatians 1:6–9; Philippians 1:15–18

### Expandable essay

How did Paul sound to critics? Scripture preserves **reported charges** — often inside Paul’s rebuttal — not a neutral outsider biography. Present them as claims in the text, not as this site’s verdict on Paul.

**2 Corinthians 10–13.** Opponents (as Paul reports them) paint him as bold in letters but weak in bodily presence, and crafty in how he “catches” the Corinthians (see esp. 2 Corinthians 10:1–11; 12:16). Paul answers with irony, catalog of sufferings, and insistence that he did not burden them — without deleting the conflict from the record.

**Galatians 1:6–9.** Another gospel is unsettling the churches. Paul anathematizes any messenger — even an angel — who preaches a different gospel. The opponents here are teachers of a circumcision-and-law gospel, not unnamed personality critics only.

**Philippians 1:15–18.** Some preach Christ “from envy and rivalry,” others from goodwill. Paul rejoices that Christ is proclaimed either way — a different texture of opposition than Galatians’ anathema, kept distinct on purpose.

**Anti-pattern:** do not flatten rivals into cartoon villains without quoting the charges, and do not treat Paul’s rebuttal as if the charges never existed.

**Deep-links:** letters 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians; city packs Corinth / Galatia / Philippi; themes on gospel and rivals if wired.

## Card: `paul-self`

**Title (keep):** Paul on himself  
**Summary (keep):** Least of the apostles / least of all saints / foremost of sinners — self-assessment set beside external praise, caution, and attack.  
**Passages (keep):** 1 Corinthians 15:9; Ephesians 3:8; 1 Timothy 1:15

### Expandable essay

**Visible chips (Critic must-fix — on card or expand header, not only mid-paragraph):**
- 1 Corinthians 15:9 — **Consensus letter**
- Ephesians 3:8 — **Consensus Prison Letter (Rome)**
- 1 Timothy 1:15 — **Consensus Pastoral**

This panel is not “others.” It is a deliberate **contrast column**: set Peter’s affirmation, James’s negotiation, Luke’s narrative, Jude’s debated echo, and opponents’ charges beside how Paul names himself.

**Consensus letter — 1 Corinthians 15:9.** “I am the least of the apostles, unworthy to be called an apostle, because I persecuted the church of God.” Undisputed Pauline voice on this atlas.

**Consensus Prison Letter (Rome) — Ephesians 3:8.** “To me, though I am the very least of all the saints, this grace was given…” Chip as **Consensus Prison Letter** with Acts 28 Rome house arrest — not bare undifferentiated “Paul” without the site’s dating/provenance note. Wider Protestant debate places some prison letters elsewhere; letter-and-road Consensus keeps Ephesians with Rome.

**Consensus Pastoral — 1 Timothy 1:15.** “Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the foremost.” Chip as **Consensus Pastoral** (same care as the Before Paul essay Critic fix). Authorship is affirmed on this atlas’s house framing; do not reopen late-authorship schemes as equal alternatives. Wider Protestant debate over Pastoral authorship exists — keep the chip honest rather than merging Timothy into the same undifferentiated voice as 1 Corinthians.

Read together: least apostle, least saint, foremost sinner — humility language that sits beside external praise (Peter), caution (hard letters), negotiation (James), and attack (opponents).
